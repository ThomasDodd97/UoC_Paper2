\section{Materials \& Methods}
Optimisation experiments carried out here involved both the practical 
 methods associated with preparation and characterisation of thermoset 
 composite samples as well as computational techniques developed to carry
 out Bayesian optimisation based on data obtained.
Furthermore, synthetic routes are presented for producing any materials
 not readily available commercially.
This section will first present practical methods including
 synthesis of itaconic acid polyester and diesters, thermoset composite
 preparation, and characterisation techniques employed.
Subsequently, the computational method by which Bayesian optimisation was
 implemented will be elucidated.

\subsection{Diitaconate Synthesis \& Characterisation}
Dimethyl itaconate was synthesised by esterification of itaconic acid
 with methanol by the Fischer method which has previously been used
 successfully in the literature \autocite{Trejbal2022,Cowie1979,Cowie1977}; 
 this is presented in Figure~\ref{fig:SimpleDMIReactionScheme} as a 
 simplified reaction scheme.
The method was adapted from the work of Trejbal et al. and involved the 
 mixing of itaconic acid in an excess of methanol in a molar ratio of 1:3
 \autocite{Trejbal2022}, whilst p-toluenesulphonic acid was used as a
 strong acid catalyst and made up 4\unit{\percent}~w/w of 
 the final reaction mixture.
Detailed practical method by which this was achieved has been placed in
 the supporting information (Sec.~\ref{subsec:NoOnSyOfDi}), but may 
 be shortly summarised: initially the reaction mixture is refluxed to
 produce the impure product, liquid-liquid extraction is then employed to
 draw out the pure product into a suitable organic solvent, and finally this
 solvent is removed under reduced pressure to yield the final product.

Several different characterisation techniques were used to monitor the 
 reaction progress, identify chemicals, and ascertain the product purity,
 including thin layer chromatography (TLC), Fourier-transform infrared 
 spectroscopy using attenuated total reflectance (ATR-FTIR), and 1H and 
 13C nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy (NMR).
Preliminary investigations undertaken using TLC have been detailed
 in the supporting information (see Sec.~\ref{subsec:NoOnThTLCOfDii}) for
 the sake of brevity.

ATR-FTIR spectra were obtained using a Nicolet iS50 machine with the 
 spectral range between 400-4000~\unit{\per\centi\metre} being investigated
 at a resolution of 0.09~\unit{\per\centi\metre}.
Figure \ref{fig:FTIRComparison} presents the spectra obtained for the pure
 reactant (itaconic acid) and compares it with that of the product
 isolated after rotary evaporation.
These data support the hypothesis that the reactant was succesfully 
 esterified to form the product dimethyl itaconate in a number of ways.

Firstly, the broad peak characteristic of O---H bond stretching commonly
 located between 2500-3200~\unit{\per\centi\metre} is clearly
 present for the itaconic acid but not for the product
 (Fig.~\ref{fig:FTIRComparison}A), suggesting the loss of O---H bonds
 and therewith the carboxyl groups associated with either the initial
 reactant (itaconic acid) or the intermediate product (monomethyl itaconate).

Secondly, strong peaks are observed around 1710~\unit{\per\centi\metre}
 (Fig.~\ref{fig:FTIRComparison}B \& \ref{fig:FTIRComparison}D) which
 is commonly indicative of C=O bond stretching (which occurs between 
 1640-1760~\unit{\per\centi\metre}); this would be consistent with C=O
 bond retention which would occur during succesful esterification.
Furthermore, it may be noted that peaks associated with carboxylic C=O bonds
 occur between 1640-1730~\unit{\per\centi\metre} whilst ester C=O peaks
 may occur between 1690-1750~\unit{\per\centi\metre}; the positive difference
 between the reactant C=O peak located at 1700~\unit{\per\centi\metre}
 (Fig.~\ref{fig:FTIRComparison}B) and the product's peak at
 1720~\unit{\per\centi\metre} (Fig.~\ref{fig:FTIRComparison}D) would
 support succesful esterification.

Thirdly, an intermediate-sized peak is observed both for the reactant
 (Fig.~\ref{fig:FTIRComparison}C) and the product
 (Fig.~\ref{fig:FTIRComparison}E) at 1630 and
 1640~\unit{\per\centi\metre} respectively, which is
 suggestive of nonconjugated C=C bond stretching (occuring between
 1620---1680~\unit{\per\centi\metre}), which would be consistent with
 retention of the double bond during the proposed esterification.
As an aside, clusters of peaks likely associated with C-H bond stretching
 were observed on both spectra
 (Fig.~\ref{fig:FTIRComparison}F \& \ref{fig:FTIRComparison}G) but
 inter-comparison was obfuscated by the strong and broad O---H stretching
 signal apparent in the reactant's spectrum.

\subsection{Unsaturated Polyester Resin Synthesis \& Characterisation}
Unsaturated polyester was synthesised via the polyesterification of
 itaconic acid with propylene glycol to produce poly(propylene itaconate),
 as described by the simplified reaction scheme in
 Figure~\ref{fig:SimpleUPReactionScheme}.
This was achieved using the melt polycondensation method, since a precedent
 exists in the literature for its succesful use in the synthesis of
 linear unsaturated polyesters derived from itaconic acid
 \autocite{Dai2017,Spasojevic2021,Pantic2022,Panic2017}.
The stoichiometric ratio between itaconic acid and propylene glycol was
 set at 1:1.1, on the basis that propylene glycol is lost during the
 heating process which reaches temperatures exceeding its boiling point
 \autocite{Parkyn1967}.

Initially, the `azeotropic method' of melt polycondensation was undertaken
 whereby a reaction vessel was charged with diols, diacids, and a 
 water-entraining agent (5\unit{\percent}~w/w mixed xylenes) before being
 heated for an extended period (approximately 11~\unit{\hour}).
Subsequently, an inhibiting agent (0.06\unit{\percent}~w/w hydroquinone)
 was added to the molten polyester before combination
 with reactive diluent (30\unit{\percent}~w/w dimethyl itaconate) to
 produce the final unsaturated polyester resin.
A more detailed description of this method may be found in the supporting
 information under Section~\ref{subsec:NoOnSyOfUnPoRe}.
As a predictor variable which was to be used during optimisation, the 
 reactive diluent content of resins produced initially was intentionally 
 made lower than optimal ratios seen in literature
 \autocite{Parkyn1967,Ahamad2001,Cherian2004,Bodnar1990}, this allowed a
 range of resins with varying amounts of reactive diluent to be tested by 
 simply mixing in more of these monomeric reagents to the initially produced
 resins.

Conventional gel permeation chromatography (GPC) was used to characterise
 the distribution of hydrodynamic volumes in solution and to estimate
 polystyrene-equivalent apparent number- and weight-average molar masses
 (\(M_{\text{n,app}}\), \(M_{\text{w,app}}\)) and dispersity 
 (\(\text{\DJ}=M_{\text{w}}/M_{\text{n}}\)).
Testing was undertaken using a Viscotec system (manufactured by Malvern
 Panalytical) consisting of both a GPCmax autosampling module as well as
 the TDA305 triple-detection module (providing a concentration detector,
 viscometer, and light scattering detector).
The column set employed consisted of a guard column followed by two
 Agilent PLgel 5~\unit{\micro\meter} MIXED-D analytical columns
 (all being held at 35~\unit{\degreeCelsius}), with
 these providing high-molecular weight resolution typically necessary
 for the study of resins.
Consistent with previous GPC work on unsaturated polyesters we used
 tetrahydrofuran as the eluent, with this suitably solvating the 
 unsaturated polyester samples obtained from the polyesterification
 reactor \autocite{Podzimek1994}.
Polyester samples were diluted to 5~\unit{\milli\gram\per\milli\litre}
 and filtered through an Agilent 0.45~\unit{\micro\meter} 
 polytetrafluoroethylene membrane before entry into the GPC columns
 at an injection volume of 100~\unit{\micro\litre}.
Five replicate injections were undertaken 
at a flow rate of 1~\unit{\milli\litre\per\minute}
 with the data presented in
 Figure~\ref{fig:SampleGPC} representing one of these experiments.

Although viscometry and light-scattering signals were recorded,
 molar-mass distributions were calculated using the conventional 
 refractive index based method with polystyrene calibration.
This was necessitated by a combination of factors
 including the absence of a known refractive index increment value,
 the heterogeneity of the polyester samples handled (obfuscating
 signal responses across detectors), and the extremely broad elution
 range.
Instead the viscometer and  light-scattering traces were used 
 qualitatively to assess the presence of large species.
All molar masses reported are polystyrene-equivalent apparent 
 values, as deduced using Agilent EasiVial PS-H calibration samples.
This conventional calibration method has previously been shown to 
 successfully approximate the number average molecular weight of 
 polyesters, with values obtained from either vapour pressure 
 osmometry or end group analysis being within 15\% of GPC 
 values \autocite{Podzimek1990}.

Figure~\ref{fig:SampleGPC} presents the results of the GPC characterisation
 of the batch of polyester synthesised, with both differential molecular
 weight fraction and cumulative weight fraction plotted against apparent
 molecular weight of chains.
The apparent distribution is multi-modal with two peaks occurring outside 
 of the calibration limits (Fig.~\ref{fig:SampleGPC}I/II) and three peaks
 identifiable within (Fig.~\ref{fig:SampleGPC}A/B/C).

Peaks I and II are caused by total permeation and exclusion respectively,
 with both being expected to be unphysical as they occur
 outside the of the styrene-standard calibration bounds, rendering
 molecular weights estimated here uncertain extrapolations.
If an arbitrary set point of 
 \(10^{5}\)~\unit{\gram\per\mole} is taken as the boundary separating
 the early eluting fraction from the rest of the trace, then 
 approximately 15\% of the cumulative weight fraction is associated 
 with the early-eluting feature at or near the column-exclusion limit.
Its accompanying viscometry and light-scattering responses are consistent
 with species of unusually large hydrodynamic volume (in excess of the upper
 calibration bound, \(MW>3.9\times10^{6}\)~\unit{\gram\per\mole}), which may 
 include highly branched species, soluble microgels or aggregates.
Consequently, calculation of number \(M_{\text{n}}\) and weight
 \(M_{\text{w}}\) average molecular weights directly from the apparent
 distribution exhibit values 
 (952 \& 1.6\(\times 10^{6}\)~\unit{\gram\per\mole}) far in excess of that 
 which might be expected for either conventional maleic-based
 (709 \& 3,382~\unit{\gram\per\mole}) or similar itaconate-based polyesters
 (600 \& 3,657~\unit{\gram\per\mole}) \autocite{Bamane2020}.
A hypothetical reconstruction of the trace excluding the early-eluting
 feature (see the supporting information in Sec.~\ref{subsec:NoOnGePeCh})
 was plotted (red dashed line, Fig.~\ref{fig:SampleGPC}).
This hypothetical trace displayed a 15\% reduction in its summed weight
 fraction relative to the apparent distribution and presented  
 \(M_{\text{n}}\) and \(M_{\text{w}}\) values (606
 \& 3,006~\unit{\gram\per\mole}) not dissimilar to those detailed in
 literature for similar itaconate based polyesters.

By discerning the existence of three separate peak structures 
 (Fig.~\ref{fig:SampleGPC}A/B/C), mathematical deconvolution was employed
 (see Sec.~\ref{subsec:NoOnGePeCh}) to split apart the apparent
 distribution into three log normal distributions allowing the estimation
 of \(M_{\text{n}}\), \(M_{\text{w}}\), weight fraction, and
 dispersity \(\text{\DJ}\) as summarised in Table~\ref{tab:SummaryOfGPC}.
Distribution C represented the largest weight fraction of the modelled 
 distributions, and exhibited a dispersity (\(\text{\DJ}=5.62\)) not dissimilar
 to that reported for similar itaconate-based polyester resins
 (\(\text{\DJ}=6.0\)) \autocite{Bamane2020}.
Such dispersity values were likely the result of an extended reaction
 time (the outcome of eschewing catalysts) coupled with the lack of
 radical inhibiting agent, which would have facilitated a high degree of
 branching.
Contrastingly, the modelled distributions A/B presented low dispersity
 values.
These two peaks were consistent with a proportion of the linear polyester
 species undergoing intramolecular cyclisation, on the basis that
 cyclised species would present as a separate peak of lower molecular 
 weight as their more compact conformation has a lower hydrodynamic volume 
 relative to a linear species of the same molecular mass.
Literature presents the occurence of these peaks in GPC analyses for 
 a variety of unsaturated polyester resins based on maleic and phthalic
 anhydride \autocite{Cristobal1993,Podzimek1990,Cristobal1991b}, whilst
 the more recent work of Molina et al. further considers the possibility of
 substantial cyclisation in itaconate-based unsaturated polyesters
 \autocite{Molina2020}.
Note that we could not rule out the contemporaneous
 elution of solvent or additive peaks within the trace.

\subsection{Composite Preparation}
Figure \ref{fig:CompositePreparation} presents an overview of the method by
 which cured thermoset composite samples of various compositions were
 prepared.
Given the danger of self-accelerating decomposition reactions and 
 associated thermal runaway, we obtained a safely filled base initiating
 agent comprised of both dibenzoyl peroxide and calcium sulphate
 (Fig.~\ref{fig:CompositePreparation}A); this arrived pre-mixed in a set 
 ratio of 2:8 between the dibenzoyl peroxide (\(1-c_{2}=0.2\) w/w) and the
 calcium sulphate (\(c_{2}=0.8\) w/w).
Base unsaturated polyester resin was mixed
 (Fig.~\ref{fig:CompositePreparation}B) in a set ratio of 7:3 between
 unsaturated polyester resin (\(c_{1}=0.7\) w/w) and dimethyl
 itaconate (\(1-c_{1}=0.3\) w/w) as described previously.
This elected ratio represents the lowest amount of reactive diluent 
 possible before increasing viscosity precluded the possibility of 
 further mixing.
This increased the size of the possible parameter space 
 to be interrogated by increasing the range of modified unsaturated 
 polyester resin reactive diluent concentrations that could be mixed 
 to by means of dilution.

Modified unsaturated polyester resin was mixed
 (Fig.~\ref{fig:CompositePreparation}C) in a varying ratio of
 \(x_{1}:(1-x_{1})\) between the base unsaturated polyester resin
 (\(x_{1}\) w/w) and the dimethyl itaconate (\(1-x_{1}\) w/w).
By varying the \(x_{1}\) parameter over the range (\([0.45,1.00]\)) we
 could produce any of the desired mixing ratios, fully describing
 the first predictor variable to be optimised (\(x_{1}\), 
 unsaturated polyester content modifier).
Modified initiating agent was mixed (Fig.~\ref{fig:CompositePreparation}D)
 in a varying ratio of \(x_{2}:(1-x_{2})\) between the base initiating agent
 (\(x_{2}\)) and calcium sulphate (\(1-x_{2}\)).
The parameter \(x_{2}\) was varied over the range (\([0.4,1.00]\)) hence 
 describing the second predictor variable to be optimised 
 (\(x_{2}\), dibenzoyl peroxide content modifier).
Initiated unsaturated polyester resin was mixed
 (Fig.~\ref{fig:CompositePreparation}E) in a varying ratio of
 \(x_{3}:(1-x_{3})\) between modified unsaturated polyester resin
 (\(x_{3}\)) and modified initiating agent (\(1-x_{3}\)); \(x_{3}\) was 
 provided a range (\([0.5,0.95]\)) which therewith described the final
 predictor to be optimised (\(x_{3}\), initiating agent content modifier).

Initiated unsaturated polyester resin samples were thermochemically cured
 into rigid thermoset plastic samples by exposing them to elevated
 temperatures in a drying oven (Fig.~\ref{fig:CompositePreparation}F); the
 curing profile was fixed for all samples: 3~hours at 
 80~\unit{\degreeCelsius} followed by 1~\unit{\hour} at
 120~\unit{\degreeCelsius}.
All cured thermoset samples were machined to the same dimensions
 (Fig.~\ref{fig:CompositePreparation}G) in preparation for characterisation;
 this was achieved by a grinding process.

\subsection{Composite Characterisation}
In order to quantify the mechanical performance of thermoset polymers 
 created using Bayesian optimisation, their apparent compressive modulus 
 (\(E_{\text{app}}\)) and peak compressive stress (\(\sigma_{\text{max}}\)) 
 were characterised using compressive testing.
Compressive testing was chosen based on both the simplicity with which 
 a curing regime could be devised for the manufacture of samples of the 
 requisite dimensions and the low mass of material required for carrying
 out tests.
The operational metrics were chosen as stress-strain curves consistently
 failed to exhibit conventional initial linear regions or a distinct yield 
 or break point.
An American Society for Testing and Materials standard was employed 
 (``ASTM D695-23'') in the devising of method, with a cylindrical test
 sample diameter of 12.7~\unit{\milli\metre} height 12.7~\unit{\milli\metre},
 and a compression speed of 1.3~\unit{\milli\metre\per\minute} adopted.
To mitigate against concerns of Euler buckling taking place in samples of
 the proposed dimensions, we calculated the critical load based on
 Euler's critical load equation (see Sec.~\ref{subsec:MeTDP} in the
 supporting information) and some
 estimated properties for conventional polyester thermosets
 \autocite{Euler1744}.
What follows is an summary of the method undertaken in the
 automated analysis of datasets derived from mechanical testing, with
 more detail provided in the supporting information
 (see Sec.~\ref{subsec:MeTDP}) .

\paragraph{Equipment}
Compressive testing was carried out using a ``Tinius Olsen 50ST''
 universal test machine (50~\unit{\kilo\newton} load capacity) fitted with
 compression platens.
Standard software (`Horizon') purveyed by the machine's manufacturer
 was used in the control of the machine, retrieval of data from the load
 cells, and export of formatted data for further analysis.
Exported data consisted of time (\(\pm\)~0.16~\unit{\micro\second}),
 force (\(\pm\)~1~\unit{\milli\newton}), and platen displacement
 (\(\pm\)~1~\unit{\nano\metre}).

\paragraph{Correction of Raw Data}
Finite compliance of the testing machine introduced an additional 
 displacement contribution into raw exported datasets.
To correct for this, a blank compression test was performed with no sample 
 positioned between the platens, generating a force-displacement curve 
 corresponding to the inherent compliance of the machine.
This curve was subsequently used as a correctional dataset.
Figure \ref{fig:CorrectionalMethod} compares the correctional dataset with
 the representative raw and corrected compression datasets.
Point~`A' on the raw dataset is transformed to point~`B' on the corrected
 dataset by subtracting the corresponding machine displacement contribution
 \(d_{\mathrm{cor}}\), from the measured displacement \(d_{\mathrm{raw}}\).

\paragraph{Processing of Corrected Data}
Load values from a sample's corrected dataset were converted into
 engineering stress values by dividing by the initial surface area.
Each cylindrical sample's diameter was measured using a ``Magnusson''
 digital caliper (accuracy \(\pm\)~0.02~\unit{\milli\metre}) from three
 different positions (\(\text{ø}_{1},\text{ø}_{2},\text{ø}_{3}\)
 in \unit{\milli\metre}), each offset by roughly +45\degree~around the
 circumference, and the mean of these values (\(\text{ø}_{\mathrm{avg}}\) in
 \unit{\milli\metre}) calculated.
Subsequently, the average top-face surface area (\(\text{ø}_{\mathrm{avg}}\))
 was obtained from the average diameter \(\text{ø}_{\mathrm{avg}}\), allowing
 the engineering stress (\(\sigma\) in \unit{\mega\pascal}) to be
 calculated (Equation \ref{eq:EngineeringStress}) by considering the
 force applied (\(F\) in \unit{\newton}) and the initial top surface area,
 \(A_{\mathrm{init}}\) \autocite{Martin2006}.
\begin{equation}\label{eq:EngineeringStress}
    \sigma _{\mathrm{eng}} =\frac{F}{A_{\mathrm{init}}}
\end{equation}

Displacement values from a sample's corrected dataset were converted into
 engineering strain by taking the difference between the initial length of 
 the samples and the length of the sample during compression.
The initial length (\(l_{\mathrm{init}}\) in \unit{\milli\metre}) of sample 
 was calculated by taking the mean of three length measurements 
 (\(l_{1},l_{2},l_{3}\) in \unit{\milli\metre}) (these taken in the same 
 fashion as for diameter).
Following this, by considering the length of a sample at any given point
 in time during a compressive test (\(l = l_{\mathrm{init}}-d\)), the
 engineering strain was calculated by finding the 
 change in sample length (\(\Delta l_{\mathrm{init}}\) in 
 \unit{\milli\metre}) and dividing this by the initial length of the
 sample (\(l_{\mathrm{init}}\)) as shown in 
 Equation~\ref{eq:EngineeringStrain}.
\begin{equation}\label{eq:EngineeringStrain}
    \epsilon _{\mathrm{eng}}=\frac{\Delta l_{\mathrm{init}}}{l_{\mathrm{init}}}=\frac{l-l_{\mathrm{init}}}{l_{\mathrm{init}}}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Stress-Strain Smoothing}
To produce a smoothed dataset (from which apparent compressive modulus and 
 peak compressive stress could be deduced) a combination of sample-rate
 reduction and filtering were applied to downsample the stress-strain dataset.
Downsampling was achieved using a windowed averaging technique, whereby
 the unsmoothed dataset was split  into a number of windows along
 the strain-axis by electing a suitable number of points per
 window.

\paragraph{Derivative Analysis}
Because the stress-strain curves did not consistently exhibit a distinct initial
 linear region, a conventional Young's modulus could not be determined robustly.
An operational apparent compressive modulus, \(E_{\text{app}}\), was therefore
 estimated from the maximum absolute local slope of the smoothed engineering 
 stress-strain curve.
The corresponding strain was located as the first inflection point of the
 stress-strain curve (equivalently, the minimum tangent modulus under the 
 adopted compression sign convention).
To find this inflection point, the first derivative of stress with respect
 to strain (i.e. tangent modulus \(E_{\mathrm{t}}\) in \unit{\mega\pascal}) was calculated
 from the smoothed stress-strain dataset by
 taking into account the instantaneous change in both stress (\(\Delta
 \sigma_{\mathrm{eng}}\)) and strain (\(\Delta \epsilon_{\mathrm{eng}}\)), and
 given by Equation~\ref{eq:TangentModulus}.

\begin{equation}\label{eq:TangentModulus}
    E_{\mathrm{t}}=\frac{\Delta \sigma_{\mathrm{eng}}}{\Delta \epsilon_{\mathrm{eng}}}=\frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{eng}}^{j+1}-\sigma_{\mathrm{eng}}^{j}}{\epsilon_{\mathrm{eng}}^{j+1}-\epsilon_{\mathrm{eng}}^{j}}
\end{equation}

Figure~\ref{fig:DerivativeComparison} shows how smoothing of the 
 stress-strain dataset facilitated the analysis of the resultant tangent
 modulus versus strain dataset.
Step-like features are apparent in the tangent-modulus curve obtained from
 the unsmoothed stress-strain data.
These may be consistent with either distretisation or internal processing
 of the exported displacement signals and subsequently amplified by
 the process of numerical differentiation.
To avoid undue physical significance being assigned to these artefacts,
 proportional-limit detection was performed upon a smoothed stress-strain
 dataset.

Detection of the first inflection point was automated using an
 algorithm that iterated chronologically through the tangent modulus versus
 strain dataset from low to high strains.
At each point, the algorithm calculates an average gradient exhibited
 by an equal number of points both before and after the current strain
 value (the `point horizon').
Using a point horizon of ten, the first inflection point was defined as
 the first point at which the summed forward and backward gradients fell
 below zero.
This point was taken as the proportional limit, as shown in 
 Figure~\ref{fig:DerivativeComparison}A.

\paragraph{Objective Retrieval}
By re-considering the smoothed stress-strain dataset, the point with the
 closest proximity to the proportional limit's determined strain value was
 equated to the first inflection point: the proportional limit
 (Fig.~\ref{fig:ExampleStressStrain}A).

As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:ExampleStressStrain}, a linear regression was
 undertaken to identify the apparent compressive modulus \(E_{\text{app}}\)
 in accordance with Equation~\ref{eq:YoungsModulusCalculation}.
The point nearest the identified strain was used to define the endpoint of a local
 fitting window.
A linear regression was fitted to the ten preceding smoothed points, and
 \(E_{\text{app}}\) was taken as the absolute value of the fitted slope.
Since the data were obtained in compression, the fitted gradients were
 negative; reported modulus values are given as absolute magnitudes.

\begin{equation}\label{eq:YoungsModulusCalculation}
    E_{\text{app}} = |(\Delta \sigma_{\text{eng}} / \Delta \epsilon_{\text{eng}})_{\text{fit}}|
\end{equation}

The peak compressive stress, \(\sigma_{\text{max}}\), was defined as the maximum
 engineering stress recorded during the test: 
 \(\sigma_{\text{max}}=\text{max}(\sigma_{\text{eng}})\).
For these curves, the second-inflection-point routine located this maximum
 reproducibly, but the quantity should not be described as yield strength or the
 onset of plasticity.

For the stress-strain curves obtained in this study, this point coincided
 with the maximum engineering stress and could therefore be identified
 reproducibly across samples.

\subsection{Optimisation}
As described above, three predictors controlled the composition of samples:
 the unsaturated polyester content ratio (\(x_{1}\)), the dibenzoyl peroxide
 content modifier (\(x_{2}\)) and the initiating agent content modifier
 (\(x_{3}\)).
These three predictors and their ranges formed a \textit{d}-dimensional 
(\(d=3\)) parameter space (\(\mathcal{X}\)) which is a subset of the entire 
 real coordinate space (\(\mathcal{X}\subseteq \mathbb{R}^{d}\)).
This parameter space was explored by the sequential optimisation techniques,
 with the objective variables including apparent compressive modulus
 (\(E_{\text{app}}\)) and peak compressive stress (\(\sigma_{\text{max}}\)).

Three different optimisation procedures were carried out from the same
 initial prior dataset: two were single-objective Bayesian routines with
 differing objectives, whilst the third was a purely exploratory method
 which was objective-indifferent.
A fourth multi-objective Bayesian optimisation routine incorporating both
 the objectives previously considered was carried out using a combination of
 the original prior dataset and all data generated by the three sequential
 optimisations.

\paragraph{Single-Objective Experiments}
Optimisation problems were based on black-box functions \(f\) which
 took any point within the parameter space \(\mathcal{X}\) and returned a real-valued
 target \(\mathbb{R}\); this being a mapping between the two respective
 spaces (\(f:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow \mathbb{R}\)).
Equation~\ref{eq:GoverningOptimisation} describes optimisation as the
 maximisation of the black-box function by tuning the predictor variable
 values (\(\mathbf{x}=[x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}]\)) such that the global optimum
 set of predictors (\(\mathbf{x}^{*}=[x_{1}^{*},x_{2}^{*},x_{3}^{*}]\)) is
 sought.
The finite experimental campaign cannot establish the global maximiser; subsequent
 results are therefore described in terms of the best observed formulation within
 the sampled design space and sample budget.

\begin{equation}\label{eq:GoverningOptimisation}
    \mathbf{x}^{*}=\argmaxA_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}} f([x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}])
\end{equation}

Equation~\ref{eq:SOBOProblems} describes the two black-box functions which
 were separately maximised during the course of the single-objective
 optimisations; the former \(f_{\mathrm{E}}\) and latter \(f_{\sigma}\) considering
 apparent compressive modulus \(E_{\text{app}}\) and peak compressive stress
 \(\sigma_{\text{max}}\) as target variables respectively.

\begin{equation}\label{eq:SOBOProblems}
    \begin{split}
        &E_{\text{app}}=f_{\mathrm{E}}(\mathbf{x})\\
        &\sigma_{\text{max}}=f_{\sigma}(\mathbf{x})
    \end{split}
\end{equation}

Eight vectors of predictor variables, each corresponding to a sample (\(i\)),
 were representatively drawn by Sobol sampling from across the parameter
 space \(\mathcal{X}\) forming an initial matrix of predictors (\(\mathbf{X}\)); the
 matrix's form is presented in Equation~\ref{eq:MatrixOfPredictors}.

\begin{equation}\label{eq:MatrixOfPredictors}
    \begin{split}
        \mathbf{X}=&[\mathbf{x}^{i=1},\mathbf{x}^{i=2},...,\mathbf{x}^{i=8}]\\
        =&[[x_{1}^{i=1},x_{2}^{i=1},x_{3}^{i=1}],[x_{1}^{i=2},...],...]
    \end{split}
\end{equation}

Evaluation of all the vectors of the predictor matrix (\(f_{E}(\mathbf{X})\)
 or \(f_{\sigma}(\mathbf{X})\)) generated a vector of targets 
 (\(\mathbf{E_{\text{app}}}\) or
 \(\bm{\sigma_{\text{max}}}\)).
Combination of the initial predictor matrix \(\mathbf{X}\) and the
 corresponding targets \(f(\mathbf{X})\) produced an initial dataset
 (\(\mathcal{D}=[\mathbf{X},f(\mathbf{X})]\)) with which sequential
 optimisation experiments were begun.
During the course of a sequential optimisation experiment, 16 further samples
 were drawn from across the parameter space \(\mathcal{X}\), with one sample taken per
 iteration; each sample's predictor vector \(\mathbf{x}\) and function
 evaluation \(f(\mathbf{x})\) was appended to the growing dataset
 \(\mathcal{D}\) associated with that experiment.
At each iteration, the Bayesian experiments strove to balance exploration and
 exploitation during selection of sample composition within the parameter
 space, whilst the benchmark deterministic experiment solely focussed upon
 exploration.

Bayesian optimisation involved an iterative process of developing a surrogate
 model approximating the latent objective function based on the available
 dataset \(\mathcal{D}\), using an acquisition function to evaluate
 candidate suitability and selection of a new sample \(\mathbf{x}^{i}\), and
 finally evaluation of a sample followed by incorporation of the resultant
 target scalar \(\sigma_{\mathrm{max}}^{i}\) and predictor vector \(\mathbf{x}^{i}\)
 into the growing dataset \(\mathcal{D}\).

On updating the dataset \(\mathcal{D}\), Gaussian process regression was used
 to develop a Gaussian process regression model \(\mathcal{GP}\) which can
 be considered a distribution over possible functions
 (\(f(\mathbf{x})\sim \mathcal{GP}(m(\mathbf{x}),K(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}'))\))
 fully described by both its mean (\(m\)) and covariance (\(K\)) functions.
Computationally, the Gaussian process was modelled by a high-dimensional
 multivariate Gaussian distribution \(\mathcal{N}(\bm{\mu},\bm{\Sigma})\)
 described by its mean vector \(\bm{\mu}\) and covariance matrix
 \(\bm{\Sigma}\); each successive iteration saw the prior process conditioned
 on new data producing an updated posterior process.

To encode our prior beliefs in process smoothness, we
 employed the Mat\'{e}rn family of covariance functions; specifically,
 one with a hyperparameter \(\nu=\)\nicefrac{5}{2}.
%  which yields
%  Equation~\ref{eq:MaternCovarianceFunction} based on Euclidean distance
%  (\(d\)).
The \(\nu=\nicefrac{5}{2}\) case balanced the need for smoothness in sampled
 paths with the drawbacks of using higher values of \(\nu\). 
As \(\nu \rightarrow \infty\), the sampled paths become infinitely
 differentiable, making them unrealistically smooth for practical
 application \autocite{Snoek2012} and potentially interfering with succesful
 calculation of the likelihood \autocite{Stein1999}.
\footnotesize
\begin{equation}\label{eq:MaternCovarianceFunction}
    \begin{split}
        K_{M\nicefrac{5}{2}}&(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x'})=(1+\sqrt{5}d+\frac{5}{3}d^{2})\mathrm{exp}(-\sqrt{5}d)\\
        &\mathrm{where}\text{ }d=|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x'}|
    \end{split}
\end{equation}
\normalsize

Each iteration saw the use of a one-step lookahead sampling policy utilising
 a simple reward utility function (Equation~\ref{eq:UtilityFunction}) which
 takes the current dataset \(\mathcal{D}\) and of all samples \(\mathbf{X}\)
 returns the sample \(\mathbf{x}\) exhibiting the highest target
 (\(\phi\); in practice either \(E_{\mathrm{app}}\) or \(\sigma_{\mathrm{max}}\)) according to the
 posterior process's mean function \(\mathrm{max}\ m(X)\).
\begin{equation}\label{eq:UtilityFunction}
    u(\mathcal{D})=\mathrm{max}\ m(\mathbf{X})
\end{equation}

Utility quantification of a putative sample \(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{p}}\) was
 calculated by the expected improvement acquisition function
 \(\alpha_{\mathrm{EI}}\) (Equation~\ref{eq:ExpectedImprovement}) which calculates
 the marginal gain in utility (\(u(\mathcal{D'})-u(\mathcal{D})\)) between
 the incumbent best target value in the current dataset (\(\phi^{*}\)) and
 that of a new dataset containing the putative \(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{p}}\) value
 (\(u(\mathcal{D'}\))).
The second line of Equation~\ref{eq:ExpectedImprovement} clarifies how the
 calculation is one for the expected marginal gain, whilst the third
 presents reformulation for the noiseless case; this equation exemplifies
 how putative points are rewarded based on both scale and probability of
 improvement effectively trading off exploitation and exploration.
Here, we applied a more sophisticated logarithmic noise-aware
 implementation (``qLogNoisyExpectedImprovement'' or ``qLNEI'') which has
 recently seen succesful use in high-dimensional computational domains
 \autocite{Ament2025}.
Given a lack of repeat measurements during characterisation, target variable
 values provided to the Ax package were modelled with inferred homoscedastic
 noise.

\footnotesize
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ExpectedImprovement}
    \begin{split}
        &\alpha_{\mathrm{EI}}(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{p}},\mathcal{D})=u(\mathcal{D'})-u(\mathcal{D})\\
        &=\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{max}\,m_{\mathcal{D}'}(\mathbf{X}')]-\mathrm{max}\,m_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathbf{X})\\
        &=\int_{\phi^{*}}^{\infty}\phi\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^{2})d\phi-\phi^{*}\int_{\phi^{*}}^{\infty}\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^{2})d\phi\\
    \end{split}
\end{equation}
\normalsize

Trivial optimisation of the acquisition function
(Equation~\ref{eq:AcquisitionFunctionOptimisation}) was achieved by
 generating a large set of Sobol sampled points across the parameter space
 \(\mathbf{X}_{\mathrm{p}}\), after which cheap evaluation facilitated identification of
 the candidate sample with the maximal utility score \(\mathbf{x}^{+}\).
\begin{equation}\label{eq:AcquisitionFunctionOptimisation}
    \mathbf{x}^{+}=\argmaxA_{\mathbf{X}_{\mathrm{p}}\in\mathcal{X}}\alpha(\mathbf{X}_{\mathrm{p}})
\end{equation}

The objective-indifferent space-filling benchmark sampling exercise was based
 on a sequential Monte Carlo strategy by the name of mc-intersite-proj-th
 (``MIPT'') \autocite{Crombecq2011}.
Utility was quantified by the conditional acquisition function displayed
 in Equation~\ref{eq:MIPT}, which took both a putative point
 \(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{p}}\) and the current dataset's previous sample coordinates
 \(\mathbf{X}\) and returns either zero or the Euclidean distance to the
 nearest previously sampled point \(k\) depending on whether projected
 distance is below or in excess of the \(d_{\mathrm{m}}\) value respectively.
The \(d_{\mathrm{m}}\) is dependent upon the number of previous samples \(n\) and the
 arbitrary constant \(s\) which has been found to be optimally set to 0.5
 for a reasonable trade-off between intersite distance and projected
 distance.
\footnotesize
\begin{equation}\label{eq:MIPT}
    \begin{split}
        &\alpha_{mipt}(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{p}},\mathbf{X}) =\\
        &\begin{cases}
            0       & if\:\min\limits_{\forall \mathbf{x}_i \in X}||\mathbf{x_{i}-\mathbf{x_{\mathrm{p}}}}||_{-\infty}<d_{\mathrm{m}}\\
            k,      & if\:\min\limits_{\forall \mathbf{x}_i \in X}||\mathbf{x_{i}-\mathbf{x_{\mathrm{p}}}}||_{-\infty}\ge d_{\mathrm{m}}
         \end{cases}\\
        &\mathrm{where}\:d_{\mathrm{m}}=\frac{2s}{n}\:\mathrm{and}\:k=\min\limits_{\forall \mathbf{x}_i \in X}||\mathbf{x_{i}-\mathbf{x_{\mathrm{p}}}}||_{2}
    \end{split}
\end{equation}
\normalsize

\paragraph{Multi-Objective Experiment}
Given the desirability of both apparent compressive modulus and 
 peak compressive stress in a thermoset composite, a dual-objective Bayesian
 optimisation experiment was attempted, with the \textit{a posteriori} goal of 
 sample-efficiently identifying the Pareto front (\(\mathcal{P}\)) 
 \autocite{Garnett2023}.
The Pareto front consists of all samples which remain unsurpassed (by any
 other sample evaluated) in terms of at least one of their target variables
 (\(E_{\text{app}}\) or \(\sigma_{\text{max}}\)).
This Pareto front allows a rational agent to make subjective yet informed
 decisions about material composition in light of expected trade-offs in
 end properties.
Equation~\ref{eq:MOBOProblem} frames the multi-objective optimisation
 problem in terms of a dual-objective function \(\mathbf{f}\) (comprised of
 the single-objective functions \(f_{\mathrm{E}}\) and \(f_{\sigma}\)) which returns
 a vector \(\mathbf{\mathrm{y}}\) of the two targets (\(E_{\text{app}}\) and 
 \(\sigma_{\text{max}}\)).
\begin{equation}\label{eq:MOBOProblem}
    \begin{split}
        \mathbf{y}&=\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x})\\
        \{E_{\text{app}},\sigma_{\text{max}}\}&=\{f_{\mathrm{E}}(\mathbf{x}),f_{\sigma}(\mathbf{x})\}\\
    \end{split}
\end{equation}

An observed Pareto front may be obtained directly from a given dataset
 \(\mathcal{D}\) using the function \(f_\mathcal{P}\)
 (Eq.~\ref{eq:ObservedPFunc}).
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ObservedPFunc}
    \begin{split}
        \mathcal{P}&=f_\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{D})\\
        &=\{\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{y}\in\mathbf{Y},\nexists\:\mathbf{y}'\in\mathbf{Y}s.t.\:\mathbf{y}'\succ\mathbf{y}\}
    \end{split}
\end{equation}

Alternatively, where a multi-objective function \(\mathbf{f}\) is
 considered alongside a matrix of predictor variables \(\mathbf{X}\), the
 function \(f_\mathcal{P}^{*}\) may be used to produce a Pareto
 front (Eq.~\ref{eq:ParetoFrontFunc}).
\small
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ParetoFrontFunc}
    \begin{split}
        \mathcal{P}&=f_{\mathcal{P}}^{*}(\mathbf{X};\mathbf{f})\\
        &=\{\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x})|\mathbf{x}\in\mathbf{X},\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x})\succ\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}')\forall\mathbf{x}'\in\mathbf{X}\}
    \end{split}
\end{equation}
\normalsize

By retrieving all the raw mechanical test data associated with the samples
 evaluated in the three sequential experiments and their common prior, a
 56-sample dataset \(\mathcal{D}\) was generated comprised of a matrix of
 sampled predictors \(\mathbf{X}\) and a matrix of their respective
 targets \(\mathbf{Y}\) (\(\mathbf{E_{\text{app}}}\) and \(\bm{\sigma_{\mathrm{max}}}\)).
Having conditioned two separate Gaussian processes on \(\mathbf{E_{\text{app}}}\) and
 \(\bm{\sigma_{\text{max}}}\) we obtained two separate posterior processes
 (\(\mathcal{GP}_{\mathrm{E}}\) and \(\mathcal{GP}_{\sigma}\)); these
 provided initial prior processes for all subsequent sequential optimisation.
The pair of resulting processes were respectively described mathematically
 by their mean (\(m_{\mathrm{E}}\), \(m_{\sigma}\)) and covariance functions
 (\(K_{\mathrm{E}}\), \(K_{\sigma}\)) and computationally by their multivariate
 normal analogues (\(\bm{\mu}_{\mathrm{E}}\), \(\bm{\mu}_{\sigma}\) and
 \(\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathrm{E}}\), \(\bm{\Sigma}_{\sigma}\)).
Equation~\ref{eq:MOBOSampledFuncs} shows how a dual-objective surrogate
 function \(\tilde{\mathbf{f}}\) may be sampled from the pair of Gaussian
 processes (\(\mathcal{GP}_{\mathrm{E}}\) and \(\mathcal{GP}_{\sigma}\)) previously
 conditioned.
\footnotesize
\begin{equation}\label{eq:MOBOSampledFuncs}
    \begin{split}
    \tilde{\mathbf{f}}\sim\:&\{\mathcal{GP}_{\mathrm{E}},\mathcal{GP}_{\sigma}\}\\
    \{\tilde{f_{\mathrm{E}}},\tilde{f_{\sigma}}\}\sim\:&\{\mathcal{GP}(m_{\mathrm{E}},K_{\mathrm{E}}),\mathcal{GP}(m_{\sigma},K_{\sigma})\}
    \end{split}
\end{equation}
\normalsize

The utility of a given Pareto front was evaluated using the hypervolume indicator
 utility function \(u_{\mathrm{hv}}\) presented in Equation~\ref{eq:UtilityHV}, which
 is equal to the \textit{d}-dimensional Lebesgue measure \(\lambda_{\mathrm{d}}\) of the space
 dominated by a Pareto front \(\mathcal{P}\) and bounded from below by
 reference point \(r\) (\(r\) and \(v\) describe vertices).
\begin{equation}\label{eq:UtilityHV}
    u_{\mathrm{hv}}(\mathcal{P};r)=\lambda_{\mathrm{d}}(\bigcup_{v \in \mathcal{P}}[r,v])
\end{equation}

Equation~\ref{eq:FunctionHVI} shows how the utility function \(u_{\mathrm{hv}}\) is
 used in the subsequent calculation of the one-step marginal gain known as
 the ``hypervolume improvement'' between the extant \(\mathcal{P}\) and the
 updated Pareto front \(\mathcal{P}'\) (which includes the putative point
 \(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{p}}\)).
\begin{equation}\label{eq:FunctionHVI}
    f_{\mathrm{hvi}}(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{P}';r)=u_{\mathrm{hv}}(\mathcal{P}';r)-u_{\mathrm{hv}}(\mathcal{P};r)
\end{equation}

Here, we use a common multi-objective acquisition function called expected
 hypervolume improvement, described by Equation~\ref{eq:AcqFuncEHVI},
 which takes a putative point \(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{p}}\) and the current dataset
 \(\mathcal{D}\) and returns the instantaneous improvement metric.
Expectation manifests itself during the speculative update of the dataset
 \(\mathcal{D}\) to \(\mathcal{D}'\), where the two targets are drawn from
 their respective marginal Gaussian distributions (of their respectively
 updated posterior Gaussian processes) located at the putative point
 \(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{p}}\) whose expectation (\(\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{p}})\)) is
 the mean of the marginal at that location
 (\(m_{E}(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{p}})\) or \(m_{\sigma}(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{p}})\)).
\small
\begin{equation}\label{eq:AcqFuncEHVI}
    \begin{split}
        &\alpha_{ehvi}(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{p}};\mathcal{D})=f_{\mathrm{hvi}}(f_{\mathcal{P}}(\mathcal{D}'),f_{\mathcal{P}}(\mathcal{D});r)\\
        &\mathrm{where}\:\mathcal{D}'=\mathcal{D}\cup\{\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{p}},m_{\mathrm{E}}(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{p}}),m_{\sigma}(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{p}})\}
    \end{split}
\end{equation}
\normalsize

This work adopted Daulton's re-formulation of the classic acquisition
 function (Eq.~\ref{eq:AcqFuncEHVI}) which takes into account the noisy
 nature of objective function evaluations \autocite{Daulton2021}. 
Although the true formulation contains an integral which is analytically
 intractable, it may be approximated using Monte Carlo integration.
Equation~\ref{eq:FunctionNEHVI} describes the integral-approximated 
 re-formulation of the acquisition function \(\hat{\alpha}\) which takes a
 putative point \(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{p}}\) and the dataset \(\mathcal{D}\) and
 returns an instantaneous improvement value.
This noisy expected hypervolume improvement acquisition function
 iterates from \(1,\dots,N\) using the \(t\) counter, with each iteration
 drawing a random dual-objective function \(\tilde{\mathbf{f}}_{\mathrm{t}}\) from
 the posterior gaussian process (see Eq.~\ref{eq:MOBOSampledFuncs}).
This function \(\tilde{\mathbf{f}}_{\mathrm{t}}\) is used to speculatively produce
 Pareto fronts (\(\mathcal{P}\) and \(\mathcal{P}'\)) for both an original
 dataset \(\mathbf{X}\) and the updated dataset
 \(\mathbf{X}\cup\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{p}}\) respectively, before the hypervolume
 improvement is gauged using \(f_{\mathrm{hvi}}\) and the many trials \(N\) averaged.
\small
\begin{equation}\label{eq:FunctionNEHVI}
    \begin{split}
        &\hat{\alpha}_{\mathrm{nehvi}}(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{p}};\mathbf{X})=\\
        &\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathrm{t}=1}^{N}f_{\mathrm{hvi}}(f_{\mathcal{P}}^{*}(\mathbf{X}\cup\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{p}};\tilde{\mathbf{f}}_{\mathrm{t}}),f_{\mathcal{P}}^{*}(\mathbf{X};\tilde{\mathbf{f}}_{\mathrm{t}});r)
    \end{split}
\end{equation}
\normalsize
This work practically applied the log implementation of the noise-aware
 \(\alpha_{\mathrm{nehvi}}\) acquisition function
 qLogNoisyExpectedHypervolumeImprovement (``qLNEHVI'') which has been shown 
 to improve Bayesian optimisation performance in noisy settings
 \autocite{Daulton2021}. 

\paragraph{Implementation}
All Bayesian experiments were implemented in the Python3 programming language, with
 functionality spanning initial sampling techniques,
 adaptive sequential sampling methods, and an experimental workflow
 management system facilitating human-in-the-loop laboratory work; this
 package is given the acronym ``BO4ACST'' (version 1.0.0)- ``Bayesian
 optimisation for atmospheric carbon sequestering thermosets''
 \autocite{HojlundDodd2025}.
Note that Bayesian sequential optimisation techniques including Gaussian
 process regression, gaussian process conditioning, and sample utility
 evaluation were facilitated by the open-source adaptive experimentation
 platform or ``Ax'' (version 1.1.2) \autocite{Facebook2025}.
Note that ``Ax'' makes use of a great deal of code from ``Botorch'',
 especially in its implementation of custom Bayesian optimisation routines
 (e.g. non-standard acquisition function implementations)
 \autocite{MetaPytorch2025B}.
