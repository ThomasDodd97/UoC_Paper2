\section{Introduction}
Thermoset composites underpin structural applications ranging from
 construction panels to transport components due to their high stiffness,
 dimensional stability and chemical resistance \autocite{Gilbert2017}.
However, unlike thermoplastics, crosslinked thermosets cannot be readily
 remelted or reshaped, and end-of-life options remain dominated by
 incineration and landfill \autocite{OWID202507121456}.
In 2019 alone, 353 Mt of plastic waste was generated
 globally, of which only 9~\unit{\percent} was recycled, while 
 thermoset-rich composite waste is increasingly directed toward energy
 recovery through combustion \autocite{OWID202507121456}.
As most commercial thermosets are derived from fossil 
 feedstocks, their incineration reintroduces fossil carbon into the 
 atmosphere, directly coupling materials performance to carbon-cycle 
 perturbation \autocite{Eriksson2009}.

Since the vast majority of the virgin plastic manufactured annually
 is thermoplastic this group has naturally dominated plastict 
 sustainability literature, with papers focused upon improvement of 
 recycling methods \autocite{Jagadeesh2022}, mitigation of microplastic 
 waste \autocite{Nafea2024},and biodegradability \autocite{Moshood2022}.
However, in part due to their relatively low industrial volume have
 received far less attention \autocite{Liu2021}.
It is generally impractical to depolymerise cross-linked thermoset
 materials which has lead to the widespread landfilling
 or incinerating of these materials at end-of-life
 \autocite{Utekar2021,Chen2023,Topham2019}.
Contemporary European legislation has prioritised incineration ahead
 of landfill for composite materials with thermosetting matrices, 
 and incoming legislation (e.g. Directives `99/31/EC' and 
 `2000/53/EC') is likely to increase the proportion incinerated
 \autocite{Pickering2006}.
As the majority of thermoset plastics are derived from fossil-based
 sources of carbon, their combustion releases the geologically 
 sequestered carbon into the atmosphere, resulting in a net addition
 of CO\textsubscript{2} into the short-term carbon cycle 
 \autocite{Levi2018}.
The present work addresses this challenge by initially identifying a
 suitable bio-based thermoset chemistry, whereafter it seeks to
 sample efficiently formulate these for maximal cured strength by a
 procedure involving Bayesian optimisation.
Here, fully bio-sourceable denotes a resin whose organic constituents
 can all be produced from biogenic feedstocks using established or
 scalable routes.
In this study, the commercial reagent batches were not independently
 certified as biogenic.

\paragraph{Bayesian Optimisation of Thermosets}
In polymer science, Bayesian optimisation has been deployed in
 both purely computational domains (e.g. optimisation of thermoplastic
 repeat units for improved glass transition temperatures in a simulated
 parameter space \autocite{Minami2019}) as well as in applied practice
 (e.g. improving compositional homogeneity during co-polymerisation
 of styrene and methyl methacrylate \autocite{Takasuka2024}).
Knox et al. applied autonomous multi-objective Bayesian optimisation to RAFT
 polymerisation, coupling a flow reactor with online NMR and at-line GPC to 
 map the Pareto trade-off between monomer conversion and molar-mass 
 dispersity \autocite{Knox2022}.
Various thermosets have been practically optimised using 
 Bayesian optimisation: Benzoxazine-based thermosets have been optimised
 with respect to six thermomechanical properties \autocite{Lu2025},
 polycyanurates have had three thermal/mechanical/hygroscopic properties
 improved upon \autocite{Xu2023}, and epoxy thermosets based on amine curing
 agents have been optimised with respect to a trio of mechanical
 properties using a dual-driver method \autocite{Zhao2024}.
In the more specific domain of practical Bayesian optimisation of
 bio-based thermosets, literature has been dominated by epoxy-based
 chemistries; Albuquerque et al. successfully improved glass transition
 temperatures in bio-based epoxy thermosets derived from amino acid based
 resin systems \autocite{Albuquerque2024}, whilst Rothenh\"{a}usler et al.
 explored this class of thermosets further by taking into account both cost
 and bio-based content \autocite{Rothenhausler2024}.

Bayesian optimisation has seen practical application in the development
 of thermoset composites with a paper by Schoenholz et al. considering
 unidirectional carbon fibre prepreg based on epoxy resin; here, both the
 layup and curing cycle variables were optimised to reduce the 
 process-induced deformation in final composite parts \autocite{Schoenholz2024}.
Despite this, relatively little literature exists concerning the practical
 application of Bayesian optimisation in the development of properties in
 polyester-based thermosets, their bio-based analogues, or their derived
 composites.
In applying Bayesian optimisation for the development of strength in
 simple composites based on itaconate-derived thermoset chemistries, 
 this paper addresses this gap in the literature.

\paragraph{Bio-Based Thermoset Chemistry}
Thermosets are formed from a wide range of different chemistries
 including (but not limited to): phenolic resins, unsaturated polyester
 resins, epoxy resins, and polyurethanes \autocite{Goodman1986}.
One prominent group are the unsaturated polyester resins, which are
 routinely used in the manufacture of composite components.
These resins exhibit desirable properties including ease of handling in
 liquid form, rapid curing, and excellent dimensional stability
 \autocite{Aziz2005,Lawrence1960}.
An archetypical unsaturated polyester resin is the mixture of 
 poly(propylene glycol fumarate) copolymer (polymerised 
 from propylene glycol and maleic anhydride) with a styrene reactive 
 diluent \autocite{Ahamad2001}.
This conventional formulation is currently manufactured from fossil
 feedstocks.
For example, propylene glycol is industrially manufactured from
 propylene oxide- a downstream chemical product of propane extracted
 from natural gas \autocite{Levi2018}.
Maleic anhydride is synthesised by catalytic oxidation
 of the chemical butane \autocite{Felthouse2001}- this derived from 
 fractional distillation of petroleum crude \autocite{Kissin1987}.
And styrene is produced from the dehydrogenation of 
 ethylbenzene- this being the product of a reaction between
 benzene and ethylene, both of which are fossil-fuel derived
 feedstocks \autocite{Lee2008,Levi2018}.

Although literature describes numerous attempts at synthesising the
 aforementioned conventional monomers from bio-based feedstocks
 (e.g. maleic anhydride \autocite{Pavarelli2015,Li2016,Teong2014} and
 styrene \autocite{McKenna2011}) only research into propylene glycol
 \autocite{Dasari2005,Ricciardi2017,Berlowska2015,Xiao2015} has
 achieved apparent success in scale-up and commercialisation, so
 we retained this monomer in the present work.
Also incorporated into this works polyester synthesis was
 itaconic acid, a simple bio-based
 difunctional carboxylic acid manufactured by fungal
 fermentation of sugars by fungi such as 
 \textit{Aspergillus terreus} \autocite{Bafana2017}.
Itaconic acid's dicarboxyl and monoalkene functionality
 allows it to directly substitute maleic acid during
 polycondensation to form an unsaturated polyester 
 \autocite{Bamane2020}.
Furthermore, itaconic acid can be synthesised into a
 variety of diesters (the family of diitaconates) which
 exhibit properties appropriate
 of a reactive diluent (i.e. radical initiated
 homopolymerisation and a low viscosity), allowing
 them to replace styrene in an eventual unsaturated
 polyester resin \autocite{Sollka2020}.

Itaconic acid's potential is clear in the literature, where
 subjects including the catalytic influence on polycondensation
 of itaconic acid within eventual unsaturated polyesters 
 \autocite{Schoon2017}, synthesis of partially bio-based resins
 incorporating itaconic-acid-based unsaturated polyester but
 retaining styrene as the reactive diluent \autocite{Costa2017},
 as well as fully bio-based resins using itaconic-acid-based
 unsaturated polyester in tandem with diitaconates as
 reactive diluents \autocite{Pantic2022,Panic2017} have been
 disseminated.
Furthermore, previous studies have considered partially bio-based
 resins incorporating itaconic-acid-based unsaturated polyesters
 together with the cross-linking compatible methacrylate class
 of reactive diluents \autocite{Spasojevic2021,Dai2017}.
Although traditionally fossil-fuel derived, recent work has seen
 bio-based methyl methacrylate synthesised from
 itaconic acid, which would unlock fully bio-based resins based
 on methacrylate reactive diluents in future \autocite{Nagai2001,Bohre2021}.
Despite inferior homopolymerisation rates \autocite{Sollka2020} and viscosity
 characteristics \autocite{Spasojevic2021} relative to methacrylates, 
 diitaconates were nonetheless focused upon in this research ahead of 
 methacrylates on account of their relatively higher strengths as cured 
 thermosets \autocite{Spasojevic2021}, safety as reactive diluents 
 (lower toxicity and flammability) \autocite{Leonard1970}, and 
 environmentally-benign nature as a non-volatile organic compound 
 \autocite{Spasojevic2021,Trejbal2022}.

\paragraph{A Thermoset Particulate Composite}
In 1997, Kharas et al. prepared several samples of bioresorbable bone
 cement composites using both polyester-based thermosets in combination
 with particulate calcium sulphate, where the latter's content was
 found to effect the final thermosets' strength and modulus
 \autocite{Kharas1997}.
The sensitivity of mechanical properties toward this variable inspired
 the present work's adoption of anhydrous calcium sulphate as a
 particulate reinforcing element during the preparation of 
 simple particulate composites based on a fully bio-sourceable
 unsaturated polyester resin.

Counterparting this particulate reinforcing, the
 thermosetting matrix was based on a cross-linked unsaturated
 polyester system; these being reliant upon a combination of
 both condensation and addition polymer.
The condensation polymer is typically an unsaturated linear polyester
 formed from the copolymerisation of diols and diacids, with at least one
 monomer having an unsaturated double bond in its structure which is
 retained after copolymerisation- this copolymer is referred to as an
 unsaturated polyester \autocite{Seraji2022}.
Whilst the addition polymer is typically generated through a radical-addition
 homopolymerisation reaction, where a monomer which had been acting as
 an unsaturated polyester solvent reacts and crosslinks individual 
 unsaturated polyester chains- these monomers are therefore referred to as
 reactive diluents \autocite{Chen2015}.
The combination of unsaturated polyester and reactive diluent is known
 as an unsaturated polyester resin; a viscous liquid, which, on addition
 of radical-initiating chemicals, can rapidly form a rigid thermoset
 material (i.e. the composite's thermoset matrix).
Previous work by Panic et al. found dimethyl itaconate to be the strongest
 contender of the diitaconates as a bio-based replacement for styrene
 in unsaturated polyester resins, given that resultant cured thermosets
 exhibited relatively higher crosslink densities (73\unit{\percent} the
 density of a styrene-based thermoset) \autocite{Panic2017}.
This work therefore sought to replicate these unsaturated polyester resins,
 where the reactive diluent was pure dimethyl itaconate and the unsaturated
 polyester was poly(propylene itaconate).

Having both described the particular material considered in the present
 work and provided a thorough review of Bayesian optimisation's utility
 in the sample-efficient formulation of thermoset materials, the paper
 will subsequently take the following form:
Initially, a detailed description of experimental methods will be
 provided spanning feedstock synthesis, bio-sourceable unsaturated
 polyester resin synthesis, and particulate composite preparation.
Furthermore, this method section provides details of the various
 optimisation procedures implemented.
Subsequently, a results and discussion section disseminates findings
 arising from both the single-objective and dual-objective routines
 undertaken, as well as cross-comparing the optimisation of a 
 model conventional resin based on styrene.
Finally, a conclusions section notes the more salient findings
 such as the successful single-objective Bayesian optimisation of
 these materials to strengths approaching those of styrene analogues.
Note that commercially available reagents were used throughout.
Their individual carbon provenance was not independently verified;
 references to a fully bio-sourceable resin describe the availability
 of biogenic production routes for all organic constituents, rather 
 than the certified origin of the specific reagent batches used.
